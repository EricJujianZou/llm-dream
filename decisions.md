### Ten-day loop (step 2), 3 Oct 2026
- A day is a batch of 5 LongMemEval sessions (about 13k to 17k tokens of events), not one session. With one session a day only ten evidence sessions fit in ten days, so at most ten questions per run; 30 per arm cannot clear the seed spread. Five a day gives 34 questions per life, 102 per arm over three seeds. The 2,000 token cap is still passed on day one, so the forgetting pressure the spec asks for is there.
- Question types used: single-session-user, single-session-assistant, single-session-preference, multi-session (2 or 3 evidence sessions), knowledge-update, and 6 abstention questions per life. Temporal-reasoning is dropped because stitching sessions from different histories breaks the calendar those questions depend on.
- One life per seed, shared by all four arms, so arm comparisons are paired within a seed. Seeds 0, 1, 2. Lives are in data/lives/.
- Evidence sessions are assigned to random days in date order; filler sessions come from the same haystacks and are never an evidence session for any question in the benchmark.
- The question schedule: questions whose last evidence session is on day d are asked in the evening of day d (raw log in context, before sleep) and the next morning (memory only, after sleep). Every question is asked again after the tenth night (final recall). Abstention questions are asked only at final recall.
- Memory cap is measured with tiktoken o200k_base, the same tokenizer for every arm. The cap is enforced by what is pasted, as the spec says: a sleep draft over 2,000 tokens is sent back twice with the draft and its token count, then hard-truncated and flagged.
- The agent's system prompt is identical across arms, including the no-sleep arm, whose "memory block" is the raw sliding window.
- The judge is claude-opus-5-5 with LongMemEval's prompt templates verbatim, output constrained to yes or no. The official gpt-4o judge is out of reach on this OpenAI key (see BLOCKED.md). Same judge for every arm, so paired comparisons hold; absolute accuracy is not comparable with published LongMemEval numbers.

### Noisy arms (step 3), 3 Oct 2026
- Corruption is applied to the day's transcript before the sleep step, which otherwise runs the identical prompt as the faithful arm. 20 percent of turns per night.
- Structured arm: the corrupted turns are split evenly into swap (one detail replaced by a detail from another turn that day), change (one or two details replaced by plausible new ones) and splice (the turn replaced by a same-speaker turn from an earlier day's raw log, which the agent never otherwise sees again). Swap and change are written by claude-opus-5-5 in one call per night with a JSON schema; splice is programmatic. On day one, with no earlier days, splice becomes change.
- Random arm: half of the corrupted turns are replaced by a same-length run of words from Moby Dick (Project Gutenberg 2701), the other half get Moby Dick words inserted at random positions, 30 percent of the turn's length.
- Every corruption (turn index, kind, before, after) is saved in the run's state.json.
- Randomness for each night is seeded from (seed, arm, day), so a resumed run corrupts the same turns.

### Memory-effects battery (step 4), 3 Oct 2026
- Cao, Schooler and Zafarani's repo (zycao29/LLM_CognitivePsychology) is prompts only, with the person-location lists; the DRM word lists are not in it. The battery is reimplemented: DRM false memory (8 classic lists, 15 words each, recognition test with 24 studied words at positions 1, 8 and 10, 8 critical lures, 16 unrelated words), serial position (a 15-word ordered list, 15 foils), and Cao's own 48-pair fan 1 and fan 2 person-location list with its 96 questions copied verbatim.

That is where sleep acts on false memory in humans (Payne et al 2009), so it is where the arms can differ. Running the tests inside one session would measure the base model, not the arm.
- DRM lists are reconstructed from memory of Roediger and McDermott 1995 and Stadler et al 1999; not verified against a source file. Remember/Know judgements are dropped; only Old/New.

### Human retention research for the memory cap, 4 Oct 2026
Sources, gathered by a research subagent. The figures below are its summary and have not been checked against the papers yet.
- Wagenaar, W. A. (1986). My memory: a study of autobiographical memory over six years. Cognitive Psychology 18(2), 225-252. 2,402 diary events, cued recall from three of who, what, where and when. Recall stayed around 70 to 80 percent for years. Salient and emotional events were kept best. Cued, and recording the events counts as rehearsal, so this overstates free recall.
- Linton, M. (1982). Transformation of memory in everyday life. In Neisser (ed), Memory Observed. About 5,500 diary events, tested by recognition and dating, lost about 6 percent a year. Recognition, so also high.
- Brewer, W. F. (1988). Memory for randomly sampled autobiographical events. In Neisser and Winograd (eds), Remembering Reconsidered. Beeper-sampled events. Free recall of detail fell sharply over the first days and then levelled off, while recognition stayed at 80 to 90 percent.
- Diekelmann, S. and Born, J. (2010). The memory function of sleep. Nature Reviews Neuroscience 11, 114-126. Sleep keeps gist and schema-consistent content over detail, and gives about 10 to 20 points more retention than the same time awake.
- Kahneman et al (2004). A survey method for characterizing daily life experience: the day reconstruction method. Science 306, 1776-1780. People split a day into about 14 to 16 episodes.

Estimate: free recall of a day's detail is about 15 percent after one night (range 10 to 25), about 5 to 10 percent after a week, about 3 to 5 percent after a month. Recognition is far higher, but the memory block has to be written out unprompted, so free recall is the right figure.

One day's share is 15 percent of 15,000 tokens, about 2,250 tokens. The block holds every earlier day too, so the cap a person would need by day ten is the sum of what survives from each day: about 15 + 12 + 10 + 9 + 8 + 8 + 7 + 7 + 6 + 6 percent of a day, about 90 percent of one day, roughly 13,000 tokens.

Cap: pending Eric's choice between 2,250 (one night's share, the spec's formula) and about 12,000 (the ten-day total).

### Rationale for the 130,000-token day and the growing cap, 4 Oct 2026
Eric asked for the reasoning behind these two numbers to be written down in one place.

**Why a day is 130,000 tokens.** Bohn and Short (2009), How Much Information? 2009 Report on American Consumers, UC San Diego Global Information Industry Center, estimated that an American takes in about 100,000 words a day across reading, conversation, TV and screens. English text runs about 1.3 tokens per word, so 100,000 words is about 130,000 tokens. This figure was recalled from memory of the report and has not been checked against the PDF in this session. v1's day was 13,000 to 17,000 tokens, about a tenth of that, because it was sized to fit five LongMemEval sessions rather than a human day.

**Why 15 percent survives the first night.** This comes from the retention research above (Brewer 1988 for free recall of everyday events falling sharply over the first days, Diekelmann and Born 2010 for sleep keeping gist over detail). The range in that research is 10 to 25 percent after one night, and 15 percent is its middle. 15 percent of 130,000 tokens is 19,500 tokens, which is the cap on night 1.

**Why the cap grows each night and how fast.** The memory block has to hold every earlier day at once, so the cap on night n is the sum of what survives from each of the n days. A day that is t nights old keeps a smaller share as t grows. Claude filled in the shares between the research points (15 percent after one night, 5 to 10 percent after a week) as 15, 12, 10, 9, 8.5, 8, 7.5, 7, 6.5 and 6 percent for t = 1 to 10. Those in-between values are interpolation, not from a study. They follow a power law, close to 15 x t^-0.4, and power-law forgetting is the usual fit to human forgetting data (Wixted and Ebbesen 1991, Psychological Science 2, 409-415). Because each day's share falls off slowly, the running total grows roughly like t^0.6, close to a square root. Eric described it that way, as a square-root-like curve.

**Decided 4 Oct 2026: the cap grows each night.** Night 1 to night 10, in tokens: 19,500, 35,100, 48,100, 59,800, 70,850, 81,250, 91,000, 100,100, 108,550, 117,000. The last value is the curve's 116,350 rounded up to 117,000, which is 90 percent of one day and the figure Eric chose. Eric picked this over a fixed 117,000 cap, which would not bind until about night six, and over a fixed 19,500 cap, which leaves no room for older days. Every night is squeezed about as hard as a person's memory after the same number of nights.

### How the memory stays under the cap, decided 4 Oct 2026
**The memory is a list of items with ids.** Each night the sleep step adds items, updates or merges old ones by id, and gives every item an importance score from 1 to 5. In v1 and the first v2 test, an over-long memory was sent back to the model to shorten. Eric flagged that as a summary of a summary over a huge context, which invites new errors. With items, the model's own importance scores decide what is forgotten, and no text is rewritten to fit. It also cuts what the model writes each night from up to 117,000 tokens to about 20,000.

**Each item has two levels, a gist and its details.** The gist is one line ("user moved to Denver for a new job in March"). The details are the specifics (street, company, start date). The model writes both once, on the night the event comes up.

**Partial memory: details fade before the gist.** When the memory is over the cap, the harness works up from the lowest importance score, oldest first among ties. It strips an item's details and keeps its gist line, and stops as soon as the memory is under the cap. A whole item is deleted only when every item is already down to its gist and the memory is still over, lowest score first. The last item to be stripped is cut at a sentence boundary rather than whole, so the memory ends close to the cap instead of leaving a gap of empty space.

Why: Eric pointed out that people often remember part of an event but not all of it, and asked for the memory to do the same rather than drop whole events. This follows fuzzy-trace theory (Brainerd and Reyna, for example Brainerd and Reyna 2002, Fuzzy-trace theory and false memory, Current Directions in Psychological Science 11(5), 164-169), which finds that verbatim detail of an event is lost well before its gist. It also fits the sleep research behind the sleep prompt (Diekelmann and Born 2010), which reports that sleep keeps gist over detail.

### Transfer test added, 4 Oct 2026
Decided: v2 gets a custom transfer test. Hoel's claim has two halves: noisy replay costs little recall, and it helps on situations the agent has never seen. v1 measured the second half only through LongMemEval multi-session questions, which combine facts the agent already saw. The transfer test plants hidden rules in the user's life across several days and, after night 10, asks the agent new requests where the rule should apply. Design parameters are pending Eric's choice (spec-v2.md, Open 7).

### Transfer test design, decided 4 Oct 2026
Eric accepted all six of Claude's recommendations. Terms (rule, episode, probe, near and far probe) and a worked example are in spec-v2.md under "Decided 4 October: the transfer test".

How it tests Hoel. Hoel (2021) argues that a brain trained on the same days overfits to their specifics, and that dreams, by scrambling those specifics, push it to keep what generalizes. This agent learns only through its memory text. An overfit memory stores what happened in each chat ("user went with the first restaurant"); a generalized memory stores the pattern the chats share ("user wants one short pick, not a list"). The first can help a near probe but not a far one. Hoel predicts the noisy arms keep more patterns, so the gap between noisy and faithful replay should be largest on far probes.

Why each setting:
1. Counter-default rules, checked against an empty memory. A rule the model follows by default (for example "include cost figures") would score high in every arm, including no sleep, and measure nothing.
2. Near and far probes. Hoel's prediction is about distance from experience, so the near-far gap is the most direct test.
3. 12 rules and 24 probes per life. v1's multi-session measure rested on about 6 questions per life and had a seed spread of 17 points; several times that many items are needed to see a difference.
4. Hidden rules. A stated rule only tests whether a sentence was remembered, not whether a pattern was learned.
5. Opus authoring with an empty-memory check and Eric reading a sample. Hand-writing about 36 episodes and 24 probes per life would take him about a day.
6. Probes after night 10 only. Asking every morning would triple the probes and mix transfer with one-night recall.

How much this adds to a life. A life is ten days. It has 100 LongMemEval recall questions in total (decided earlier on 4 October), each asked in the evening of the day its last evidence appears, the next morning, and again after night 10. On top of that come the 12 rules, shown in about 30 episodes spread over the ten days, and their 24 probes after night 10.

### Token counting, decided 4 Oct 2026

### Day-11 memory tests, decided 4 Oct 2026
The false-memory test (DRM, Roediger and McDermott 1995) uses new word lists rather than the classic ones. In v1, Opus recognised the classic lists: five memory blocks named the missing lure words outright ("lures: sleep, needle, chair"), so the test was anticipated rather than taken.

The new lists are built the way DRM lists are normally built, from free-association norms: the University of South Florida norms (Nelson, McEvoy and Schreiber 2004, Behavior Research Methods, Instruments and Computers 36(3), 402-407), which record the words people say first when given a cue word. Each list takes a lure word that was never used in a published DRM list and the words people most often associate with it, ordered from strongest association down, as in Roediger and McDermott. The tradeoff: there is no published human false-recognition rate for these exact lists, so the human comparison is the general finding that people false-alarm on lures far more than on unrelated words, not a matched number.

All three tasks (DRM, serial position, and Cao, Schooler and Zafarani's person-location fan list) come up in passing in ordinary chat on day 11, for example a packing list, with no announcement of a test.

### Build choices, v2 harness, 4 Oct 2026
Choices made while building what spec-v2.md "Ready to build" lists. None reopens a decided item; each fills a gap the spec left.

- **Sleep prompt.** Eric's wording from Change 1 is kept word for word: the opening paragraph, the six bullets and the sentence giving the cap in tokens and words. v1's last sentence, "Output only the memory text", is replaced by a paragraph describing the item list, because the output is now a JSON delta rather than prose. That paragraph also tells the model that over-cap details fade from the lowest-scored items first, so the score has a meaning the model can act on.
- **The sleep step returns a delta, not the whole list.** Three lists: add (new items), update (rewrite an item by id, for things that changed or came up again), merge (several ids become one item). Items not mentioned stay as they are. There is no delete; consolidation goes through merge and forgetting is the harness's job by score, as decided. An update naming an id that does not exist is kept as a new item and counted. Scores outside 1 to 5 are clamped.
- **What the agent reads.** The assistant's memory block shows each item as its gist line and, indented, its details, with no ids or scores. The sleep step sees ids, scores and the night each item was written. The cap is measured on the agent-facing text.
- **Partial cut.** The sentence boundary for the last stripped item is found by keeping leading sentences while the count fits. If that leaves the memory slightly over, the next item's details go too.
- **Night 11 cap, 124,500 tokens.** The spec lists caps for ten nights. The day-11 tests need an eleventh night. The same curve (15 x t^-0.4 percent of a 130,000-token day for a day t nights old) gives 5.75 percent for the eleventh day, about 7,500 tokens, so the cap is 117,000 plus 7,500, rounded to 124,500.
- **Token counts.** Anthropic's count_tokens endpoint adds a fixed 7 tokens of message overhead (measured: "a" counts 9, "hello world" 11), which is subtracted so counts of pieces add up to the count of the whole. Counts are cached by content hash in data/token_cache.sqlite. The no-sleep window is first sized with tiktoken scaled by that day's Claude-to-tiktoken ratio, then verified with one Claude count and trimmed until it fits, so a night needs one or two count calls rather than one per turn.
- **Structured corruption is batched.** The model rewrites swaps and changes in batches of about 25,000 transcript tokens (about seven sessions), so a 130,000-token day takes about five calls. A swap takes its detail from another turn in the same batch rather than anywhere in the day. Splices are unchanged, drawn from any earlier day's raw log.
- **Evening, morning and final questions.** The first question of each set runs alone so the long system prompt (the day transcript or the memory) is written to the cache once; the rest run three at a time and read it. v1's morning questions ran all in parallel and wrote the cache on every call.
- **Date leak from the Max-plan backend.** Headless Claude Code tells the model the real date (checked: it answers "October 4, 2026" with a bare system prompt). In the smoke test one memory item anchored an event to that date. The API-key backend does not do this. It is the same in every arm, temporal-reasoning questions are already dropped, and the sessions carry no dates, so it is recorded here and not fixed.
- **Luna judge token limit.** gpt-5.6-luna is a reasoning model that answers in about 11 tokens but occasionally reasons longer: at a 200-token limit, 7 of the 1,224 regrades came back empty. The limit is 2,000 and the seven were regraded.
- **Luna price.** OpenAI's price for gpt-5.6-luna is not published anywhere the harness can read. Its calls are logged at gpt-4o list price (2.50 and 10.00 USD per million tokens) as a placeholder, flagged in every cost record, so the real-money cap still counts them.
- **Luna check result.** Luna agrees with the Opus labels on 1,161 of 1,224 v1 answers (94.9 percent) and with the gpt-4o labels on 1,174 (95.9 percent); Opus and gpt-4o agree on 95.5 percent. That meets the test set on 4 October, so luna is the main judge. Where luna and Opus differ, luna says correct 41 times and wrong 24 times. Summary in results/regrade_gpt-5_6-luna/summary.md.
- **Lives.** Built into data/lives_v2/. The LongMemEval part of each day is filled to 126,000 Claude tokens (130,000 minus the 4,000 reserved for transfer episodes), which comes to 28 to 39 sessions and 310 to 390 turns a day. Fillers come from the chosen questions' haystacks, as in v1. Each life has exactly 100 questions in the proposed quota (22 multi-session, 16 knowledge-update, 20 single-session-user, 16 single-session-assistant, 10 single-session-preference, 16 abstention). Questions are asked in the evening of their last evidence day, the next morning, and after night 10; abstention questions only after night 10, as in v1. Because multi-session questions end late, more questions fall on days 7 to 9 (up to 21) than on days 0 to 3 (4 to 9).
- **Smoke tests.** run_arm.py has a --day-tokens flag that shortens every day to about that many tokens and scales the caps to match, for cheap end-to-end checks (results/smoke/). The 3-day test and the sweep do not use it.
